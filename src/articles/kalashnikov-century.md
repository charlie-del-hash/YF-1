No one knows how many Kalashnikovs there are. The estimate most often cited — on the order of a hundred million rifles in the AK family, against perhaps eight million M16s — is an accounting of a phenomenon that outran accounting decades ago, produced in state arsenals on four continents, copied without licence, dug out of caches, inherited, resold, and reissued through wars that outlived every government that shipped them. The Kalashnikov is the most manufactured firearm in history, and the margin is not close.

The interesting question is not how good the rifle is. It is how a mid-century Soviet infantry carbine became a permanent feature of the world.

## Designed for the worst soldier on the worst day

The AK-47's engineering brief, as reconstructed from its choices, was humility. Its long-stroke gas piston slams a heavy bolt carrier through its cycle with enormous surplus energy; its moving parts rattle in clearances a Western armourer of 1947 would have called sloppy. Every one of those choices trades marksmanship at the margin for function in the mud. The rifle assumes dirt, neglect, cold, a frightened conscript with hours of training — and works anyway. Its controls are crude and its sights are basic, and it can be taught to an illiterate teenager in an afternoon, which, in the second half of the twentieth century, is precisely what happened, hundreds of thousands of times.

## The state gives away its rifle

Design explains why the AK works; policy explains why it is everywhere. From the mid-1950s the Soviet Union transferred the design the way it transferred steel mills — as fraternal industrial equipment. Warsaw Pact states tooled up under licence; China's Type 56 alone runs to tens of millions; Egypt, North Korea and others followed. Entire national production lines were a foreign-policy deliverable, and once a state owns the tooling, the tooling outlives the state's intentions. The rifles made for the Cold War's client armies became the stock-in-trade of every conflict after it — carried out of collapsing arsenals in Albania and Iraq, priced like a commodity in the arms bazaars of Peshawar and Mogadishu, marked up and down with the local temperature of war.

{% pullquote "C. J. Chivers, The Gun" %}The Kalashnikov marks the guerrilla, the terrorist, the child soldier, the dictator and the thug — all of whom have found it to be a ready equalizer against morally or materially superior foes.{% endpullquote %}

## Symbol status

No other industrial product of the Soviet Union achieved anything like the rifle's cultural reach. It appears on Mozambique's flag and Hezbollah's crest; it has been cast in gold for dictators' gifts and painted by Basquiat; its silhouette is a global logogram that reads, depending on the audience, as liberation, menace, or simply power. Kalashnikov himself lived long enough to feel the weight of all of it — publicly the decorated patriot who always said he had built a weapon to defend the Motherland, privately the old man who wrote to the Russian Orthodox Patriarch in his final year asking whether the deaths his rifle caused were on his soul. The Patriarch's office reassured him. History's verdict is less tidy: the answer to "what did the AK do" is a large share of the small-war dead of seventy years.

## The rifle as infrastructure

The Kalashnikov century's real lesson is about durability — not of steel, but of stock. Small arms are the one weapon class where production is effectively permanent: a rifle made in 1965 kills as well in 2026, and tens of millions of them are still in circulation with centuries of service life remaining. Arms-control regimes built for missiles and tanks barely touch this; the AK's supply curve cannot be recalled, only slowly diluted. The design will be obsolete eventually. The rifles will not. Whatever the wars of the coming decades are fought over, a Kalashnikov will be present, and it will work.
