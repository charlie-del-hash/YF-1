The clichéd image of the First World War — whistles, ladders, and a line of men walking into machine guns — was obsolete before the war ended, and the army that made it obsolete was the one that lost. Between 1915 and 1918 the German army worked out, by experiment under fire, most of what the twentieth century would come to call infantry tactics: the squad as the unit of attack, fire and movement, bypassing strongpoints, artillery that suppresses rather than obliterates. In the spring of 1918 the new method tore the Western Front open from St. Quentin to the Marne. Five months later the army that invented it was finished.

## A laboratory called Rohr

The revolution began as a workshop problem. In March 1915 the Prussian War Ministry formed an experimental assault detachment to test a light infantry gun; under its second commander, Hauptmann Willy Rohr, the unit became something far more consequential — a school for a different grammar of the attack. Rohr's men wore body armour they soon discarded and carried grenades in sacks; they attacked in loose groups, hugging the ground, working around resistance rather than against it, with machine guns, trench mortars and flamethrowers pushed down among the riflemen instead of held behind them.

The insight was organisational as much as tactical. The rifle company of 1914 was a firing line commanded by officers; Rohr's assault squad — the *Gruppe* of eight to ten men around a light machine gun — was a team led by an NCO, trusted to find its own way forward. Authority moved downward, to the men who could actually see the battle. Frontline units rotated through Rohr's courses; whole divisions were later rebuilt around what they learned.

## Bruchmüller's five hours

The infantry method needed an artillery method to match, and it arrived with a retired colonel recalled from convalescence. Georg Bruchmüller — *Durchbruchmüller*, "breakthrough-Müller", to the troops — replaced the week-long destructive bombardments of 1916 with something closer to a fire plan in the modern sense: a few hours of precisely sequenced fire, mixing gas and high explosive, aimed less at killing the defenders than at severing them — from their guns, their headquarters, their sense of what was happening. Batteries registered silently by survey and calculation, so the first shell of the preparation was also the first warning.

At Riga in September 1917, then at scale on 21 March 1918, the combination proved itself: five hours of orchestrated chaos, then assault divisions moving through fog past positions whose occupants were still in their dugouts, deaf and blind.

{% pullquote "Timothy Lupfer, on the 1918 doctrine" %}The Germans changed their doctrine not because they had new weapons, but because they were willing to learn faster than their enemies from the weapons everyone had.{% endpullquote %}

## The offensive that ate its own spearhead

Operation Michael gained more ground in a week than the Allies had gained in three years of offensives, and it is precisely here that the cautionary half of the story begins. The storm troops could break any position, but a breakthrough is a logistical event as much as a tactical one: the assault divisions outran their artillery, their supply columns, and their own replacements, on ground the retreating British had stripped bare. The best-trained infantry in the German army — concentrated, by design, in the attack divisions — were exactly the men being spent at the highest rate. And the offensive had no operational object worth its cost: Ludendorff, pressed on the point, answered that he forbade the word "operation" — "we will punch a hole and see what develops."

What developed was exhaustion. By summer the trench divisions left holding the line were the army's residue, and when the Allies — who had been taking their own notes on suppression, combined arms and deep battle — struck back at Amiens in August, the front that had produced the storm troops caved in around them.

## The longest afterlife

Tactically, almost everything about the modern infantry platoon is descended from the 1918 method: the NCO-led squad built around an automatic weapon, fire and movement, the assumption that the attack flows around resistance. The German army of the next war built its infantry — and, with engines added, its armoured doctrine — directly on this foundation, and every army since has taught some dialect of it. The operational lesson travelled less well, and it is the one worth keeping: a tactical system, however brilliant, is not a strategy. Breaking the front is not the same as winning the war — a distinction the men who perfected the break understood too late for it to matter.
