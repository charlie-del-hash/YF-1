The war began below radar coverage, at helicopter height, in the dark of the Iraqi western desert. At 0238 local time on 17 January 1991, eight AH-64 Apaches of Task Force Normandy — led to their targets by Air Force Pave Low helicopters, because in 1991 only the Pave Lows had the navigation kit for it — opened fire on two early-warning radar sites with Hellfire missiles, rockets and guns. Both sites were dead within minutes. Between the wreckage lay a lane of darkened sky pointing north-west, and through it, already inbound, ran the strike packages of the largest opening night in the history of air warfare.

What followed over the next five hours was watched in two ways that had never been available before: by commanders, through the infrared gun-camera and targeting tapes that would define the war's imagery; and by the public, live, through a night-vision camera propped in a Baghdad hotel window. Air war had become video. It was a fitting way to watch a campaign that was itself, at bottom, a contest of pictures — one side fighting inside a functioning image of the battle, the other inside noise.

## KARI, the centralised brain

Iraq's air defence was not a rabble. KARI — the French contractors' name for it, "Irak" reversed — tied some 700 combat aircraft, thousands of anti-aircraft guns and a national web of radars and SAM batteries into a hierarchy of hardened sector operations centres reporting to Baghdad. Around the capital itself the gun and missile density exceeded anything the US had faced over Hanoi. But the system's strength was its weakness: it was centralised by design, built to let a suspicious regime keep its own air force on a leash. Kill the sector centres and the telephone exchanges that linked them, and the guns would still fire — furiously, blindly, upward — while the system behind them dissolved into local reflexes.

That was the plan, and it was a familiar one. Nine years earlier the Israeli Air Force had run it against nineteen SAM batteries in Lebanon's Bekaa Valley in an afternoon: make the radars light up, kill them with anti-radiation missiles, cut the controllers off from their fighters. Riyadh's planners scaled the same logic to a nation of eighteen million. The Bekaa had been the rehearsal; this was the tour.

## Three minutes past three

At 0300, as the first Tomahawks threaded into the city and F-117s released laser-guided bombs into the roofs of the sector centres, the international telephone exchange and the regime's command sites, Baghdad's gunners opened up all at once — tracer rising in red lattices over the rooftops, filmed from the ninth floor of the Al-Rashid Hotel by a CNN crew narrating the end of the pre-war world by candlelight. The line the correspondents kept repeating — that the sky over the city was lit like a fireworks finale — described, precisely, an air defence system firing at an enemy it could no longer see.

The deception layer arrived with the second wave. Drones and gliding decoys, launched to mimic strike aircraft, marched across Iraqi radar scopes; the surviving SAM batteries that rose to the bait were struck by a stacked queue of anti-radiation missiles — the SEAD barrage the planners privately called the night's real main effort. Iraqi radar operators learned within days that turning on a transmitter was a form of suicide, and mostly stopped. The guns kept firing at memories.

## The tape war

The overnight briefings made the campaign's icon not the aircraft but the recording. Grainy green-white FLIR footage — a crosshair drifting onto a rooftop air shaft, a bomb entering the frame from the top, a building exhaling — played to briefing rooms and then to the world, teaching the public a new grammar of war: the target bracketed, the impact silent, the aircraft never seen. The tapes were honest as far as they went; they were also a curated fraction of a campaign in which most bombs dropped were unguided. But they announced, accurately, where air warfare was going. From 1991 onward, precision would be the expectation, and the camera would always be running.

By dawn on 17 January the coalition had flown on the order of 1,300 combat sorties and lost a handful of aircraft — one of them, painfully, the F/A-18 of Lieutenant Commander Scott Speicher, the night's only air-to-air loss. Iraqi fighters that rose were shot down by F-15s fighting, as at the Bekaa, inside a complete picture fed by AWACS; most of the air force that survived the first week eventually fled to Iran. Six weeks of bombardment later, the ground war lasted one hundred hours.

## What the first night settled

Militaries study the Gulf War's opening hours the way an earlier generation studied 9 June 1982, and they take the same warning from both: the modern air battle is decided substantially before the first dogfight, in the contest between one side's picture of the battle and the other's. Moscow's theorists, who had coined the phrase "military-technical revolution" watching the Bekaa, saw Desert Storm as the proof at scale — and every serious air-defence system built since, from the S-300's mobility onward, is in some measure an answer to that night: disperse, shoot, move, and never, ever leave the radar on.
