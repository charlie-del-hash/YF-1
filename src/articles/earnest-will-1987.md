The tankers were the problem nobody could ignore by 1987. For three years Iraq and Iran, deadlocked ashore, had been burning each other's oil trade at sea — Iraqi jets hunting tankers lifting Iranian crude, Iran answering against the shipping of Iraq's Gulf bankers, above all Kuwait. Hundreds of merchant ships had been hit. Insurance rates said what governments would not: the world's oil artery was becoming a war zone. Then Kuwait asked both superpowers, with exquisite timing, whether they might protect its tankers — and once Moscow offered to lease it ships, Washington's answer was never in doubt.

The mechanism was a lawyer's masterpiece and a sailor's headache. Eleven tankers of the Kuwait Oil Tanker Company were re-registered under the American flag, given American names and American paper captains, and thereby became, in law, American ships entitled to American protection. On 24 July 1987 the first convoy formed up off the Strait of Hormuz: two reflagged giants, three US warships, callsigns and formation diagrams the Royal Navy of 1943 would have recognised at a glance. The US Navy, which had spent forty years building itself around carrier aviation and open-ocean war with the Soviets, was back to walking tankers past minefields at sixteen knots.

## Eighteen miles from Farsi Island

It took Iran one morning to expose the plan's soft spot. At 0700 on that first transit the lead tanker *Bridgeton* — 400,000 tons, drawing more water than anything else in the column — ran onto a contact mine eighteen miles southwest of Farsi Island, an anchorage of the Revolutionary Guard's small-boat navy. The blast tore a hole the size of a house in her port side; her honeycomb of cargo tanks kept her afloat and moving. What happened next became the operation's founding embarrassment: the escorts, warships worth a billion dollars between them and without a single minesweeper in theatre, tucked into line *astern* of the wounded supertanker. The ship they had come to protect was now the only vessel present that could survive a mine, and so she led. Tehran called it a divine blow; the Pentagon called it a temporary capability gap. Both were right.

The gap got filled the unglamorous way. Ocean minesweepers were towed and lifted to the Gulf; mine-hunting helicopters flew in; and the Navy leased two enormous construction barges, *Hercules* and *Wimbrown VII*, anchored them in the northern Gulf, and turned them into something the fleet had not owned since Vietnam — mobile sea bases, home to patrol boats, SEALs, and the Army's night-flying special-operations helicopters of Task Force 160, borrowed for an operation nobody had imagined needing them.

## The night war

Iran's answer to the convoys was calibrated to live below the threshold of war: mines laid at night from innocent-looking hulls, Revolutionary Guard speedboats swarming out of Farsi and the oil platforms, Silkworm missiles on the coast. The American answer was to go out into the dark after them. On the night of 21 September 1987, helicopters from Task Force 160 — flying blacked out, on night-vision goggles, from a frigate's deck — caught the landing ship *Iran Ajr* rolling mines off her stern into the convoy track. Rockets and guns stopped her; at first light SEALs went aboard and photographed the rest of her cargo, mines of the same series that would later be pulled out of the water along the routes. It was the rarest thing in maritime grey-zone war: evidence, in daylight, with serial numbers.

The pattern — provocation, then a measured, targeted reply — held for a year. When a Silkworm fired off the Faw Peninsula hit the reflagged *Sea Isle City* at anchor off Kuwait in October, blinding her American master, the response was not a strike on Iran proper but Nimble Archer: four destroyers methodically demolishing the Rashadat platforms the Guard used as radar pickets and boat bases, after a warning to evacuate. The message ran both ways. The escort war stayed a war of platforms, mines and small boats — right up until April 1988.

## One afternoon of real war

On 14 April 1988 the frigate *Samuel B. Roberts*, returning from an escort run, struck a freshly laid mine in a field sown across a route the Navy had swept months before. The blast broke her keel and put her main spaces underwater; five hours of damage control by her crew kept her afloat. Divers recovered other mines nearby with Iranian serial numbers matching the *Iran Ajr*'s stock — evidence again — and four days later the US Navy delivered the reply it had been rehearsing for a year. Operation Praying Mantis opened with the destruction of two platform bases and, by nightfall, had become the largest American surface engagement since the Second World War: the missile boat *Joshan* sunk after firing the last Harpoon ever launched at a US ship, the frigate *Sahand* sunk, her sister *Sabalan* left burning but afloat — spared, on orders, as a final calibration. Roughly half of Iran's operational navy was gone in an afternoon, and the mining of the convoy lanes effectively died with it.

Then, eleven weeks later, the operation acquired the stain it never removed. On 3 July 1988 the Aegis cruiser *Vincennes*, in a running gunfight with Guard boats inside Iranian territorial waters, read an airliner climbing out of Bandar Abbas as an attacking fighter and shot down Iran Air Flight 655 with 290 people aboard. Every navy that now operates air-defence systems in crowded littorals studies those seven minutes; the escort war's proudest lesson — that discipline and graduated force can keep a tanker war from becoming a real one — was written the same summer as its most terrible counter-example.

## What the convoys taught

Earnest Will ended on 26 September 1988, a month after the Iran–Iraq ceasefire, having walked roughly 130 convoys through the world's most watched strait. After *Bridgeton*, no escorted ship was lost. The ledger reads almost quaint now — until the chart is updated. Reflagging as a legal instrument, sea-based special operations against small-boat and mine campaigns, graduated retaliation against maritime grey-zone pressure, the impossible arithmetic of cheap threats against billion-dollar escorts: every line of it has been relitigated since 2023 in the Red Sea, where another coalition discovered that convoying is a discipline navies forget in peacetime and relearn under fire. The Gulf sailors of 1987 would recognise all of it — including the part where the hardest question is not how to fight, but how much.
