The missile-warning centre is a theatre built for an audience that hopes the show never opens. Its stage is the big board — the wall display where, if the day ever comes, the tracks will crawl up from the horizon with time-to-impact counters beside them. Everything about the room is engineered to compress decision time: minutes from detection to assessment, minutes more to a national command decision. In the early 1980s, on both sides of the Cold War, the room kept malfunctioning in the same direction. The board would light up. The attack would be real on every screen. And it would not be happening.

## Two, then 220, then 2,200

The American rehearsals came first. Just after midnight on 9 November 1979, displays at NORAD's Cheyenne Mountain complex, at Strategic Air Command and at the Pentagon's National Military Command Center began showing a mass Soviet launch — submarine missiles first, then ICBMs, a textbook decapitation strike. Interceptors scrambled from three bases. The president's airborne command post launched (without the president). At consoles across three command centres, assessment officers did the one thing the system was built to let them do: they checked the sensors themselves. Satellites and radars, queried directly, saw a quiet planet. Within about six minutes the event was declared false. The cause was almost unbearably mundane — a training scenario, a simulated attack tape, had found its way into the live network.

Seven months later it happened again, twice in four days, with a purer cause: a failed chip in a communications multiplexer, a part worth less than a dollar, began writing the digit 2 into message fields that should have read zero. Warning displays announced 2 inbound missiles, then 220, then 2,200. Pacific Command's bomber crews started engines; Minuteman crews brought up launch checklists. Again the direct sensor query said: nothing. After the second event, engineers found the chip. Senator Charles Percy, who happened to be at SAC headquarters during one alert, described the atmosphere as one of practised, terrifying calm.

## Reliability: highest

The Soviet rehearsal came at the worst possible moment. By September 1983 the superpower relationship was at its coldest since the Cuban crisis: the KGB was running RYaN, a standing intelligence requirement to detect preparations for a NATO first strike, and on 1 September Soviet air defence had destroyed Korean Air Lines Flight 007, killing 269 civilians and convincing each side the other was capable of anything.

Twenty-five days later, just after midnight Moscow time on 26 September, the alarm sounded in Serpukhov-15, the bunker south of Moscow that downlinked the Oko early-warning satellites. The system reported a missile launch from the American ICBM fields. Confidence: highest. Then a second launch, a third, a fourth, a fifth. The board said LAUNCH; the klaxon said act; the procedure said report an attack up the chain, where the flight clock would leave the leadership minutes to decide on retaliation.

The duty officer that night, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov, reported a system malfunction instead. His reasoning was human through and through: a real first strike would come as hundreds of missiles, not five; the ground radars that should have picked up the tracks minutes later saw nothing; and the young network — operational barely a year — had been temperamental before. He later said he was never certain, and simply refused to be the man who was wrong in the catastrophic direction. The inquiry found the cause within months: near the autumn equinox, sunlight glancing off high-altitude clouds at the terminator had aligned with the satellites' viewing geometry and read, to the software, as five rising plumes.

## The exercise that read as real

The autumn had one more act. From 2 November, NATO ran Able Archer 83, an annual command-post exercise rehearsing the procedures for nuclear release — that year with new communications formats, radio silences, and participation planned at head-of-government level. Through the RYaN lens, an exercise practising the paperwork of nuclear war looked uncomfortably like cover for the real thing, which was precisely what Soviet planners assumed a NATO first strike would use. Warsaw Pact intelligence flights surged; nuclear-capable Soviet aircraft in East Germany and Poland stood at readiness with weapons loaded until the exercise ended on 11 November. How close it truly came remains argued — but a 1990 US advisory-board review, declassified in 2015, concluded the war scare was real, and that Washington had underestimated it at the time.

## The man in the loop

The lesson of 1979–83 is not that the machines failed; machines fail. It is that every failure was caught by the part of the system that its designers trusted least — a human being with minutes on the clock, cross-examining the technology and finding its story not quite believable. A tape is too perfect; a chip counts in absurd leaps; five missiles is the wrong number for doomsday. Every subsequent debate about warning, automation and launch authority — up to and including today's, with hypersonic weapons compressing the clock and machine learning offering to do the assessing — is a debate about whether to keep that sceptical night-shift officer in the loop, and whether he will always be there when the sunlight lines up with the clouds.
