The British Expeditionary Force went to war in 1914 with an artillery doctrine built on shrapnel — airbursting shells that scythed men in the open — and an ammunition scale calculated for a short, mobile war. By the spring of 1915 the war was neither short nor mobile. The Germans were underground, behind wire, and the shell that mattered now was high explosive, which Britain's arsenals barely made. The gap between what the guns needed and what the factories produced was about to become the first great procurement scandal of the industrial age — and the template for every one since.

## A general looks for an alibi

The crisis broke as these things usually break: through failure looking for a culprit. The offensive at Neuve Chapelle in March burned through more shells in thirty-five minutes than the army had planned for a campaign; by the time of the assault on Aubers Ridge in May, batteries were rationed to a few rounds per gun per day, and the attack died against uncut wire in a single morning at a cost of eleven thousand men. The commander-in-chief, Sir John French, facing questions about his own generalship, gave the story to the press instead — briefing Charles à Court Repington, military correspondent of The Times, and sending two of his staff to London with the documents to put before Lloyd George and the opposition.

Repington's dispatch of 14 May was, by the standards of what followed, restrained: the attack had failed for "want of an unlimited supply of high explosive." Lord Northcliffe, owner of The Times and the Daily Mail, was not restrained. A week later the Mail put the blame on the war secretary by name — Lord Kitchener, the most revered soldier in the empire — under a headline about "the tragedy of the shells." Readers burned the paper in the street. The government did not survive the month regardless.

## The wizard and the ministry

The coalition government formed on 25 May carried a new invention: a Ministry of Munitions, with Lloyd George at its head and powers no British peacetime state had ever claimed. The ministry could commandeer factories, direct labour, suppress strikes and cap profits; it built national shell and filling factories by the hundred and staffed them with a workforce that eventually included close to a million women. Lloyd George ran it as a public performance of energy — "men of push and go" was the job description — and the performance mattered: munitions output became the visible measure of whether the state was serious.

The numbers turned. The famine of 1915 became the glut of 1917, when British guns at Passchendaele could fire in days what the whole of 1914 had produced. The corrective came with its own scandals — the Somme opened in 1916 with a bombardment thick with duds from rushed American and British contracts — a reminder that quantity ramps arrive before quality does.

## The rails every crisis runs on

Strip the Edwardian costume off the shell scandal and the mechanism is instantly recognisable. A peacetime establishment sizes its ammunition industry for the war it prefers to imagine. A real war consumes the stockpile in weeks. Commanders and ministers exchange blame through friendly journalists; the press turns a logistics problem into a legitimacy problem; the government pays with offices or its life. Then the state does what only the state can do — guarantees demand for years ahead, finances plant, reaches into the labour market — and discovers that shell output responds to poured concrete and trained hands, not to communiqués, on a lag of roughly two years.

Every phrase in that paragraph has appeared, lightly translated, in coverage of the West's 155 mm crisis since 2022. The men of 1915 at least had the excuse of encountering the problem first. The pattern has now run, in recognisable form, for over a century: democracies let their arsenals wither in peace, relearn the price in war, build a ministry, and promise never again. The promise has a measured half-life of about one generation.
