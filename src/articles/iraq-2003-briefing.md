Every war leaves behind a characteristic document. The First World War left the field manual; the Second left the mimeographed operations order; Vietnam left the reporting cable and the body-count table. The Iraq War of 2003 left the slide deck. The invasion was argued, revised, sold, rehearsed and briefed as PowerPoint — and when historians went looking for the campaign plan afterwards, what the archives yielded was not a plan in the Second World War sense at all. It was slides: hundreds of them, compartmented under the codename Polo Step, bullet points and arrow charts through which the most consequential military decision of a generation had flowed.

The medium was not incidental. Slide-ware had colonised American staff culture through the 1990s precisely because it was good at what the moment demanded — compressing a sprawling plan into twenty minutes of a busy principal's attention, keeping options visually parallel, letting a secretary of defence interrogate troop numbers on a chart and watch them fall, iteration by iteration, from half a million towards something politically bearable. By 2002 the invasion of Iraq existed chiefly as a deck that senior officers updated the way earlier generations had redrafted orders. What a deck does brilliantly is show a plan's shape. What it cannot do — the point everyone would relearn expensively — is hold a plan's assumptions.

## The machine that worked

None of this stopped the opening act from being a masterpiece of the systems the slides described. The war began early and off-script on the night of 19–20 March, when perishable intelligence put Saddam Hussein at a compound called Dora Farms: two F-117s and roughly forty cruise missiles were re-tasked in hours, a strike assembled at briefing speed. It missed — he almost certainly was never there — but the machinery that could improvise it had been forty years in the building. When A-day proper came on 21 March, the air campaign delivered around 1,700 sorties in a night against a national command system whose operators, blinded and pre-broken by a decade of containment and days of leaflets, largely declined to fight. There was no Iraqi radar picture to speak of, no functioning integrated defence — the Bekaa's lesson and Desert Storm's, arrived at terminal scale.

The numbers, tabulated afterwards in CENTAF's own after-action spreadsheet, tell the story of a transformed force: two-thirds of all munitions expended were precision-guided, against under a tenth in 1991; eight hundred Tomahawks; ground columns moving so fast the air plan kept re-cueing around them. Three weeks from the berm to Baghdad. As a demonstration of the networked, briefed, synchronised force the decks depicted, the march upcountry was close to flawless.

## The part after the applause

The trouble was filed under Phase IV — military planning's label for what happens after decisive operations. Phase III, the march, existed in the decks in loving detail: axes, timings, logistics, decision points. Phase IV existed as end-state bullets: Iraq territorially intact, threats removed, a government "broadly representative". The Third Infantry Division's own after-action review said it without varnish: there was no Phase IV plan — higher headquarters had issued nothing an executing unit could treat as one. The gap was not that nobody thought about the aftermath; studies existed in drawers across Washington. It was that the medium through which the war was actually decided had no way to represent the aftermath's hard questions, and so — briefing by briefing — they slid off the screen. An assumption on a slide looks identical whether it is load-bearing or decorative. *Post-hostilities transition to civil authority* fits on one line either way.

{% pullquote "H.R. McMaster, on the medium of modern staff work, 2010" %}It's dangerous because it can create the illusion of understanding and the illusion of control.{% endpullquote %}

## The genre hardens

What 2003 revealed, the following years institutionalised and then satirised. Officers spoke of "death by PowerPoint" as a garrison joke that had migrated into theatre; a spaghetti diagram of Afghan stability grew so notorious that a commanding general could quip that the war would be won when the slide was understood. A brigadier who had fought in both Iraq wars banned the format from his planning sessions entirely, on the argument quoted above. The critique was never really about software. It was about what a command culture chooses as its unit of thought — and what a bullet point, which asserts without arguing and connects without committing, quietly does to plans, to dissent, and to the ability of a subordinate to say *this line is doing more work than it can bear*.

## The document of record

Seen from the arc this journal has been tracing — the Bekaa's consoles in 1982, the gun-camera tapes of 1991 — the deck of 2003 completes a progression: each war's decisive picture moving further from the battlefield. The radar scope showed the battle; the FLIR tape showed the weapon's view of it; the slide showed the *idea* of it, sixty-eight per cent precise and one hundred per cent persuasive. The invasion those slides described was won in three weeks and vindicated the machine that made them. The war that followed for eight years was, among other things, the bill for everything the format had allowed its authors not to say. Armies still run on briefings — they always have. The lesson of 2003 is narrower and harder: know, at every echelon, which of your documents is actually the plan, and make sure it is not the one with a slide number in the corner.
