The Syrian missile crews in Lebanon's Bekaa Valley had spent the spring of 1982 doing what Soviet doctrine told them to do: dig in, camouflage, wait. Nineteen batteries of SA-2, SA-3 and mobile SA-6 missiles sat in a belt across the valley floor, pointed at the aircraft of the Israeli Air Force. A decade earlier, in the opening days of the Yom Kippur War, batteries much like these had shot the IAF out of the sky over the Suez Canal and the Golan. The missile, most of the world's air forces had concluded, had beaten the aeroplane.

On the afternoon of 9 June 1982 the Israelis took roughly two hours to demonstrate the opposite. By nightfall most of the Syrian batteries were burning, and in the days that followed the Syrian Arab Air Force lost fighters at a rate no air arm had suffered since 1945 — commonly credited as more than eighty aircraft destroyed in air combat for no Israeli losses in kind. The precise ledger is still argued, as such ledgers always are. The shape of the result is not.

## The debt of 1973

Mole Cricket 19 was conceived as the repayment of a specific debt. In October 1973 Egyptian and Syrian air defences — layered SA-2, SA-3, SA-6 and the ZSU-23-4 gun system — had inflicted losses on the IAF severe enough to change its behaviour over the battlefield. Israeli pilots who dived below the missile envelope met radar-directed guns; those who climbed above the guns met missiles. More than a hundred Israeli aircraft were lost in nineteen days, most of them to ground fire.

The lesson the IAF drew was institutional, not tactical: the air defence system had to be fought *as a system*. Radars, launchers, command posts and the communications between them were a single organism, and the organism could be blinded, deceived and then killed. Through the late 1970s Israel assembled the pieces quietly — remotely piloted decoys to make the radars light up, anti-radiation missiles to ride the beams down, airborne early warning to watch the whole board, and jamming to cut the ground controllers off from their fighters.

## Nineteen batteries in two hours

The opening move on 9 June belonged to machines without pilots. Israeli decoy drones crossed into the valley flying profiles designed to look like strike aircraft; Syrian fire-control radars switched on and, in some cases, fired. Behind the decoys, reconnaissance drones recorded each emitter's position and passed it back in something close to real time. Then came the hard kill: anti-radiation missiles launched from aircraft standing off outside the engagement zones, followed by strike packages with cluster and general-purpose bombs, with long-range artillery joining against the nearer sites.

Overhead, E-2C Hawkeyes tracked the Syrian response while heavy jamming fell across the ground-control network. Syrian doctrine leaned on ground-controlled intercept: fighter pilots were vectored by voice from the ground. When the voices dissolved into static, the MiGs kept coming anyway — into a wall of F-15s and F-16s that could see them from a hundred miles out.

## The turkey shoot

The air battles of 9–11 June were less a duel than a processing line. Syrian MiG-21s and MiG-23s, deprived of ground control and early warning, entered the fight blind; Israeli fighters, cued by the Hawkeyes and fighting with all-aspect missiles, engaged on their own terms. Dogfights that began beyond visual range frequently ended before the Syrian pilots knew they had begun. Where the fighting did close to gun and short-range-missile distance, the Python 3 and AIM-9L gave the Israelis a first-shot advantage in almost any geometry.

Syrian courage was not the variable — Israeli pilots afterwards remarked on how doggedly the MiGs pressed in. The variable was information. One side fought inside a functioning picture of the battle; the other fought inside noise.

## What Moscow saw

The strategic audience for Mole Cricket 19 sat far from Damascus. Soviet marshals had built their European air defence concept on the same family of systems and the same ground-controlled doctrine that had just failed in Lebanon, and the scale of the failure fed an argument already running inside the Soviet general staff about a "military-technical revolution" — the fear that reconnaissance, precision weapons and electronic warfare, tied together in real time, were making mass alone obsolete. Within a year Moscow had rushed SA-5 batteries and Soviet crews to Syria to restore a measure of deterrence.

For everyone else, the Bekaa became the textbook case of what would later be called a systems-of-systems fight: the week that drones, jammers, early-warning aircraft and fighters stopped being separate tools and became one weapon. Nineteen years after 1973, over Iraq in 1991, a coalition air force would run the same play at continental scale.
