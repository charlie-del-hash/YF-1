Before February 2022, the United States manufactured roughly fourteen thousand 155 mm artillery shells a month, and nobody in Washington thought the number remarkable. It was, by the logic of the previous thirty years, a rational figure: precision weapons had made mass obsolete, wars would be short, and stockpiles were a cost centre. Ukraine burned through that monthly output in a matter of days.

What followed has been the great procurement story of the decade — the rediscovery, at ruinous expense, of an idea every quartermaster of 1916 took for granted: ammunition is not a commodity you buy, it is an industry you keep alive.

## Fourteen thousand a month

The shortage was not an accident; it was a design choice. Western ammunition plants were consolidated, mothballed or sold off in tranches after 1991, each closure individually sensible and collectively catastrophic. Explosives production concentrated in a handful of sites; the machines that forge shell bodies became bespoke items with year-long lead times; the skilled trades that ran them retired without successors. When governments arrived after 2022 with money in hand, they discovered that cash does not compress chemistry — a new nitrocellulose line takes years to build and certify no matter how urgent the requirement.

The ramp has happened, but slower and dearer than the communiqués promised. Washington's stated goal of one hundred thousand shells a month has slipped repeatedly; the European Union's famous pledge of a million rounds inside a year was delivered late and partly from stockpiles rather than new production. Meanwhile Russia — with help attributed by Western and South Korean intelligence to millions of North Korean rounds — has kept its guns fed by accepting quality problems the West would not.

## Magazine depth as strategy

The deeper shift is conceptual. For three decades Western force planning asked *how good* a weapon was; the first question now is *how many* — and how fast the line behind it can run. "Magazine depth" has migrated from wargaming jargon into ministerial speeches. Multi-year procurement contracts, once resisted as inflexible, are prized precisely because they are inflexible: a guaranteed order book is what convinces a manufacturer to pour concrete.

{% dinkus %}

None of this is new. The British "shell scandal" of 1915 brought down a government and created a Ministry of Munitions; the United States spent 1940 discovering that its arsenals could not equip its own army, let alone Britain's. The pattern is constant: democracies disarm their industries in peace, pay a premium to rebuild them in crisis, and promise never to do it again. The 155 mm shell — unglamorous, unchanged in principle since Passchendaele — is simply the current unit of measurement for that promise.

Whether the promise holds this time will be visible not in white papers but in floor space: the plants under construction today are the honest signal of what governments actually believe about the length of the wars they expect.
