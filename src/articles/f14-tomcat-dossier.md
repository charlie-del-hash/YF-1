The Tomcat exists because a compromise failed. In the 1960s Washington tried to buy one swing-wing aircraft for both services; the Navy's version, the F-111B, came out too heavy and too sluggish to live on a carrier deck, and the admirals killed it in 1968. But the requirement that produced it — kill Soviet naval bombers and their cruise missiles before launch range, far from the carrier group — did not die. Grumman's answer paired the F-111B's long-reach weapon system with an airframe designed by fighter people. The prototype flew in December 1970; the fleet had it by 1974.

## A missile truck that could dogfight

The heart of the aircraft was the AWG-9 radar and the AIM-54 Phoenix missile — a combination that could track two dozen contacts and engage six of them at ranges beyond a hundred miles, decades before such numbers became routine. No other fighter of its era carried anything comparable. The airframe wrapped around it refused to be a mere truck: the variable-sweep wing gave the Tomcat honest agility at low speed and Mach-2 dash at altitude, and Vietnam-era lessons put a 20 mm cannon in the nose.

Its weakness was the engines. The TF30 turbofans inherited from the F-111 programme were prone to compressor stalls at high angle of attack — in exactly the corners of the envelope a fighter pilot lives in — and were implicated in a long list of mishaps until the F110-powered B and D models arrived in the late 1980s.

## Fleet defender, then bomber

For twenty years the Tomcat's job was the outer air battle: standing between the carrier and regiments of Soviet bombers. Its actual combat record in US hands was leaner and stranger — two Libyan Su-22s downed over the Gulf of Sidra in 1981, two Libyan MiG-23s in 1989, reconnaissance and strike escort over Iraq and the Balkans. When the Soviet bomber threat evaporated, the community reinvented the jet as the "Bombcat", adding LANTIRN targeting pods and precision bombs; some of the type's hardest working years were its last, over Afghanistan and Iraq. The US Navy retired the Tomcat in September 2006.

## The Persian Tomcats

The aircraft's second life began with its only export customer. In 1974 the Shah's Iran ordered 80 Tomcats with Phoenix missiles; 79 had been delivered when the 1979 revolution cut the pipeline, and the type passed — along with its American-trained crews — to the new Islamic Republic. Through the Iran–Iraq War the F-14 flew as interceptor and improvised early-warning platform, its big radar policing whole sectors; Iranian crews claim a substantial air-to-air record for it, though the figures remain contested and Western accounting is far more conservative.

Kept airborne for decades by cannibalisation, local overhaul and reverse-engineered parts, a shrinking number remained in nominal service at Isfahan into the 2020s — the last flying Tomcats anywhere. Even that afterlife is now closing: open-source imagery from the June 2025 Israeli air campaign showed Tomcat airframes destroyed on the ground. Half a century after the type's first flight, the aircraft built to defend American carriers ended its career defending Iranian airspace against the F-35.
